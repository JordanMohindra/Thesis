\chapter{Accounting for the Hardware Numbers}
\label{ch:interpret}

Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reported many hardware numbers, and Kiem's thesis
reports a comparable set for his design \cite{kiem2025}. Put side by side, the
two sets differ on almost every line: resources, clock, latency, throughput.
A difference on its own explains nothing. This chapter's job is to account for
every one of them: to say what each number represents, what it should be
compared against, and, wherever our design and Kiem's differ, exactly why.

The accounting is done in three steps, and each step rests on a measurement
rather than an argument.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Put the numbers on the same footing.} Kiem's resource and clock
figures are measured after Vivado has placed and routed the circuit. Every hardware figure in
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} was first obtained as an HLS estimate, made before the
circuit existed. We therefore ran the full Vivado implementation on all four
designs (Section~\ref{sec:hlsvsimpl}). The estimates turned out to be wrong by
a factor of three in logic, and wrong about whether the designs meet timing.
\item \textbf{Rebuild the reference in our tool flow.} Two designs built with
different tools, for different devices, from different code differ in many ways
at once. To separate them, we rebuilt Kiem's design from his description, in
our tool, for our device (Section~\ref{sec:replica}).
\item \textbf{Change one thing at a time.} Starting from our design and ending
at the rebuilt reference, we changed one design decision per step and
implemented every step (Section~\ref{sec:abl}). The change in each number at
each step is the cost of that one decision.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
The rest of the chapter then reads each metric against the yardsticks that give
it meaning: the sensor, the system's time budget, the link and the device, as
well as Kiem's design where the comparison is like-for-like.

\section{Two ways of counting the same circuit}
\label{sec:hlsvsimpl}

HLS reports what it \emph{expects} a circuit to cost, before the circuit has
been built. Vivado reports what the circuit \emph{did} cost, once it has been
synthesised into device primitives, optimised, placed and routed. Kiem's
figures are the second kind; ours, at first, were the first.

\subsection{Running the implementation}

\texttt{vitis-run --mode hls --impl} takes the RTL that HLS generated and runs
the complete Vivado flow on it: logic synthesis, optimisation, placement,
routing and post-route reporting. It is the flow AMD calls ``export and
implement'', and its reports are the same kind of number as Kiem's Table~5.3.
Four lines were added to each design's configuration file and nothing else was
changed:
\begin{center}
\small
\texttt{vivado.flow=impl}\qquad
\texttt{vivado.clock=10}\qquad
\texttt{vivado.report\_level=2}\qquad
\texttt{vivado.max\_timing\_paths=10}
\end{center}
Each design is implemented as a standalone block (``out of context'')
constrained to a \SI{10}{\nano\second} clock on the same
\texttt{xcku5p-ffvb676-2-e}. One run takes about \SI{45}{\minute}. It leaves
behind a post-route utilisation report (total and per module), a timing report
with the worst paths, and a power report; the script that reads all of them
into this chapter's tables is listed in Appendix~\ref{app:repro}.

Three things stand out.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Logic was over-estimated by about three times.} HLS predicted
\num{115736} LUTs for DRHE-$n$; the routed design uses \HW{drhen}{LUT}. The
other designs are similar. Every conclusion in earlier drafts that rested on
DRHE being ``2.5 times Kiem's LUTs'' rested on the estimate.
\item \textbf{Both designs meet \SI{100}{\mega\hertz}.} HLS estimated
\SI{81.88}{\mega\hertz} for DRHE and \SI{85.43}{\mega\hertz} for LPC. After
routing, DRHE-$n$ closes at \HW{drhen}{fmax}\,MHz and LPC-$n$ at
\HW{lpcn}{fmax}\,MHz. The timing conclusion first drawn in
Section~\ref{sec:phase3} reported an estimate, not a fact.
\item \textbf{DSPs and block RAM were estimated almost exactly.} These are
fixed blocks with known sizes, and HLS allocates them itself, so there is
little for the later tools to change. The exceptions are the two LPC designs'
block RAM, where Vivado re-mapped some memories (LPC-$n$: \HW{lpcn}{hBRAM}
estimated, \HW{lpcn}{BRAM} used; LPC-1,2: \HW{lpcone}{hBRAM} estimated,
\HW{lpcone}{BRAM} used), and two DSPs in LPC-$n$.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Why the logic estimates were so far off}

The per-module breakdown for DRHE-$n$ in Figure~\ref{fig:estimate} shows where
the difference comes from. It is not spread evenly across the design.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_estimate.pdf}
\caption[HLS estimate against implemented LUTs, per module]{LUTs of each part
of DRHE-$n$: the HLS estimate (from the module's synthesis report) against the
routed design (from the hierarchical utilisation report). The pre-built
floating-point adders and multipliers were estimated well. The arctangent and
the loop body, which HLS builds itself from C++, were over-estimated by factors
of about three and six. The square root and the integer-to-float conversions
were not estimated at all.}
\label{fig:estimate}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{The loop body.} Most of the estimate sat in the logic HLS generates
directly from C++: the bit packer, the S4 and Huffman lookup, the residual
arithmetic and the control. Each of the eight inserts into the output buffer is
written as \texttt{(ap\_uint<512>)code << bit\_count}, and HLS costs every one
of them as a complete 512-bit barrel shifter, about \num{2171} LUTs. Because
\texttt{bit\_count} is a signed \texttt{int}, it also adds a second shifter in
the opposite direction for negative shift amounts, and a 512-bit select between
the two. Vivado sees what HLS does not: the value being shifted is at most 29
bits wide and zero everywhere else, so most of every shifter's inputs are
constant and the logic behind them disappears. The whole loop body is estimated
at about \num{60000} LUTs and implemented in about \num{9900}.

\paragraph{The arctangent.} \texttt{hls::atan2(float, float)} is built by HLS
from generic C++ --- a CORDIC on a 43-bit internal fixed-point format with range
reduction around it --- rather than from a pre-characterised core. HLS
estimated each instance at \num{20129} LUTs; each implemented instance uses
about \num{5980}. The same effect, smaller, applies to \texttt{sin} and
\texttt{cos}.

\paragraph{What HLS did not count.} The square root and the integer-to-float
conversions are AMD floating-point IP cores that use no DSPs, and HLS reports
them as zero LUTs. Implemented, they cost \num{1945} LUTs between them. This is
the one place where the estimate is too low.

\paragraph{The clock.} HLS estimated a critical path of
\SI{12.213}{\nano\second} through an integer-to-float conversion chained into a
multiply. That figure is HLS's own model of each operator's delay, scheduled
against a budget that already reserves \SI{2.7}{\nano\second} (27 per cent of
the period) for clock uncertainty; it is not a timing analysis of real wires.
After routing, the worst path in DRHE-$n$ is \HW{drhen}{cpimpl}\,ns, starting in one of the floating-point sine/cosine units, and it passes the
\SI{10}{\nano\second} constraint with \HW{drhen}{wns}\,ns to spare.

\paragraph{What this means for the rest of the thesis.} HLS estimates are
useful for what they were designed for: comparing one version of a design
against another in the same tool, quickly. They are not a substitute for
implementation when the comparison is with a design that was implemented. From
here on, every hardware number compared with Kiem is a post-route number.

Two limits on the post-route numbers should be kept in mind. First, the designs
are implemented out of context. Vivado therefore does not know the clock tree
of a real system and cannot include its skew; the timing figures are slightly
optimistic compared with an in-system build like Kiem's. Second, the power
figure uses Vivado's default assumption that every signal toggles on
\SI{12.5}{\percent} of clock cycles, not activity recorded from real data, so it
is good for comparing designs with each other but is not a prediction of a
particular system's power.

The frame latency from co-simulation is exact for the circuit simulated, but it
measures a specific situation: the testbench feeds the frame back-to-back, one
beat per cycle whenever the design will accept it. So \num{61253} cycles is the
time the compressor would take if an entire frame were waiting for it at once.
The real sensor does not deliver data like that, which is why
Section~\ref{sec:latency} separates this number from the latencies the system
actually experiences.

\section{The yardsticks}
\label{sec:yardsticks}

Six things can give a hardware number meaning:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The sensor.} This is how fast data arrives and how much time
passes between ramps and between frames. It sets the throughput requirement.
\item \textbf{The system budget.} This is how long the rest of the processing
chain can afford to wait for compressed data. Kiem derived one explicitly.
\item \textbf{The link.} This is how many bits per second the output
connection can carry. It sets the compression ratio requirement.
\item \textbf{The device.} This is how much of the chip the design uses, and
whether it would fit on a smaller one.
\item \textbf{Kiem's implementation.} This is the only published hardware
DRHE, but it is valid only where the two numbers measure the same thing.
\item \textbf{Internal baselines.} These are the design's own theoretical ideal,
the no-prediction coder, and the entropy bound.
\end{enumerate}

sensor model used in this chapter therefore agrees with the published one.

\section{Rebuilding the reference design}
\label{sec:replica}

\subsection{Why a replica is needed}

A comparison of two numbers explains something only if the two numbers differ
in one known way. Our DRHE and Kiem's differ in at least six ways at once: the
arithmetic (floating point against fixed point), the bit packer, how the
predictor state is reset, the feedback structure (closed against open loop),
the tool version (Vitis 2026.1 against 2022.2) and the device (a
\texttt{xcku5p} at speed grade $-2$ against an \texttt{XCZU3CG-SFVC784-1-e} at
speed grade $-1$). Set side by side, the two resource tables cannot say how
much of any difference comes from which cause.

The way out is to hold everything constant except one thing at a time, and that
needs Kiem's design inside our tool flow. His source code is not published, so
we rebuilt it from the description in his Section~4.3.4, which is detailed
enough to do so:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Data types.} Magnitude \texttt{ap\_ufixed<16,16>}, phase
\texttt{ap\_fixed<16,3>}, input \texttt{ap\_fixed<16,16>}, intermediate phase
arithmetic \texttt{ap\_fixed<32,4>}, all with rounding.
\item \textbf{Functions.} \texttt{hls::sqrt} on \texttt{ap\_uint<32>};
\texttt{hls::atan2} on \texttt{ap\_fixed<16,16>}; \texttt{hls::sin} and
\texttt{hls::cos} on \texttt{ap\_fixed<16,3>}.
\item \textbf{State.} Five arrays of $4 \times 1024$ entries, partitioned by
channel, driven from the \emph{input} sample (open loop).
\item \textbf{Packing.} The real and imaginary codes of one channel packed
into one \texttt{ap\_uint<58>}; the four channel words then combined into the
256-bit output buffer.
\item \textbf{Schedule.} All four channels in parallel, $\mathrm{II} = 1$.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
Where his text leaves a choice open, the replica takes the simplest reading.
One such choice needs stating. Kiem does not describe how the predictor state
is cleared at the start of a frame, but he reports $\mathrm{II} = 1$, and
Section~\ref{sec:acc_ii} shows that $\mathrm{II} = 1$ is only possible if the
clearing does not add a third memory access per cycle. The replica therefore
clears the state by reading it as zero on the first ramp, which is equivalent.

\subsection{One source, four switches}

The replica and our design are built from one source file with four
compile-time switches (\texttt{drhe\_abl\_compress.cpp}, Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lp{0.37\textwidth}p{0.37\textwidth}@{}}
\toprule
Switch & 0: our design (DRHE-1) & set: Kiem's design\\
\midrule
\texttt{ABL\_PACK}  & eight inserts into a 512-bit buffer with a signed offset &
1: the same with an unsigned offset; 2: 58-bit word per channel, one 232-bit
insert\\
\texttt{ABL\_RESET} & state zeroed by a write on ramp 0 & state read as zero on ramp 0\\
\texttt{ABL\_ARITH} & single-precision floating point & Kiem's fixed-point types and functions\\
\texttt{ABL\_LOOP}  & closed loop: state from the reconstruction & open loop: state from the input\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\noindent
With every switch at zero the source is, statement for statement, DRHE-1: it
synthesises to the same schedule (II~2, depth 58, 40 BRAM\_18K, 132 DSPs) and
writes exactly as many bits as DRHE-1 on every one of the 50 frames. With
every switch set it is the replica. All the packing modes write the same bits, so one decoder reads them all, and the decoder follows the arithmetic
switch. Every variant was decoded losslessly on real ColoRadar frames in C
simulation, so each one is a working compressor, not a sketch.

\subsection{How close is the replica to Kiem's published design?}
\label{sec:replicaval}

Table~\ref{tab:replica} sets the implemented replica beside Kiem's published
figures.

\input{tab_replica}

The replica agrees with Kiem's design where the design is fully specified.
Block RAM matches exactly: \HW{ablk}{BRAMT} tiles against his 10, because both
store twenty arrays of \num{1024} 16-bit words (Section~\ref{sec:acc_bram}). The
DSP count is \HW{ablk}{DSP} against 44. Both designs achieve $\mathrm{II} = 1$
and both meet \SI{100}{\mega\hertz} on a speed grade $-1$ device.

The replica does \emph{not} reproduce Kiem's LUT and flip-flop counts. It needs
\HW{ablk}{LUT} LUTs and \HW{ablk}{FF} flip-flops where Kiem reports \num{45892}
and \num{9243}. His design, as he describes it, does not need the difference.
Section~\ref{sec:acc_lutff} tests the explanations his text leaves open ---
the speed grade, the packer he describes in one sentence, and his deeper
pipeline --- by building each into the replica. Together they account for
about half of the flip-flop difference and almost none of the LUT difference;
what remains most likely lies in the tool version (Vitis HLS 2022.2, whose
fixed-point math library is four years older than the one used here) or in
logic his thesis does not describe.

This matters for how his LUT figure is used. It measures one particular build;
it is not a measure of what the DRHE algorithm costs. When the question is
``what do our design decisions cost?'', the fair yardstick is the replica,
built in the same tool, for the same device, from the same description, and the
next section uses it that way. When the question is ``how does our IP compare
with the IP Kiem built?'', his published figure is the right one.

\subsection{Block RAM: the width of a word}
\label{sec:acc_bram}

\paragraph{The difference.} DRHE-1 uses \HW{drheone}{BRAMT} block-RAM tiles;
Kiem's design uses 10.

\paragraph{The accounting.} Block RAM holds DRHE's predictor state: five values
per range bin and channel from earlier ramps (the magnitude prediction, the
previous magnitude, the phase prediction and the two previous phases).
Partitioned by channel, as both designs do, that is $5 \times 4 = 20$ separate
arrays, each \num{1024} words deep. Table~\ref{tab:memtable} lists how each
design stores them.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering

holds \num{1024} words of up to 18 bits; a 36\,Kb tile is two of them.}
\label{tab:memtable}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccccc@{}}
\toprule
Design & Arrays & Words & Bits & BRAM\_18K per array & Total tiles\\
\midrule
DRHE-1 (float)          & 20 & \num{1024} & 32 & 2 & 20\\

\bottomrule

\noindent
Every DRHE-1 state word is a 32-bit \texttt{float}. A \num{1024}-word array of
32-bit words is \SI{32}{\kilo bit}, too much for one BRAM\_18K, so each array
takes a full 36\,Kb tile: twenty arrays, twenty tiles. Kiem's words are 16-bit
fixed point (\texttt{ap\_ufixed<16,16>} and \texttt{ap\_fixed<16,3>}); a
$1024 \times 16$ array fits in one BRAM\_18K, so twenty arrays need ten tiles.
The replica, built with his types, uses exactly ten. \textbf{The factor of two
is the word width, and nothing else.}

DRHE-$n$ keeps one copy of the state per transmitter, \num{1536} words per
array instead of \num{1024}. HLS rounds the depth up to the next power of two
and gives each array four BRAM\_18K, so the design uses 40 tiles: four times
Kiem's, of which two is the word width and two is the per-transmitter state.
The memory is \SI{67}{\percent} full (\num{983040} bits stored in
\num{1474560} bits of block RAM); the rounding of the depth to a power of two
and the unused parity bits are the only waste.

\paragraph{The accounting.} Every DSP in DRHE-1 belongs to an identifiable
floating-point operator, and the routed hierarchy report counts them exactly

\noindent
A single-precision multiply needs three DSP slices because its 24-bit
significand product does not fit one $27 \times 18$ multiplier; an adder uses
two, for the alignment shift and the add; the sine/cosine unit uses thirteen for
its polynomial and range reduction. In fixed point, a product of two 16-bit

Four channels at two operations each would need eight sine/cosine units and
four arctangents. DRHE-1 has four and two, because at $\mathrm{II} = 2$ each
unit is idle every other cycle and HLS shares it between two channels. Step~3
of the ladder removes that sharing: at $\mathrm{II} = 1$ the same float design
needs \HW{ablptwor}{DSP} DSPs. \textbf{The factor of three is floating-point
arithmetic; without the sharing that $\mathrm{II} = 2$ allows, it would be
six.}

\subsection{Pipeline depth: which operations wait for which}
\label{sec:acc_depth}

\paragraph{The difference.} DRHE-1's pipeline is 58 cycles deep; Kiem reports
23 (his HLS estimate, the same kind of number).

\paragraph{The accounting.} The HLS schedule report lists, for every operation,
the pipeline stage it runs in. Grouping the operations of one sample by what
they do gives Figure~\ref{fig:sched}(a). The pipeline is two chains laid end to
end. The first is the prediction: load the state, run the two IIR filters, wrap
the phase, take the sine and cosine, rescale to Cartesian and round, which ends
around stage 31. The second is the polar conversion that updates the state:
convert the sample to floating point, take $re^2 + im^2$ and its square root,
and take the arctangent, which alone occupies 28 stages. In DRHE-1 the second
chain \emph{cannot start until the first has finished}, because it converts
the reconstructed sample, and the reconstruction needs the prediction. That is
the closed loop introduced in Section~\ref{sec:defect32768}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_sched.pdf}
\caption[Pipeline schedule of one sample]{The pipeline schedule of one sample,
read from the HLS schedule reports. (a) DRHE-1 as built: the state is updated
from the reconstructed sample, so the polar conversion (red) waits for the
prediction (blue) and the depth is 58. (b) The same float design with the state
updated from the input sample (open loop): the two chains run side by side and
the depth is 32. Kiem's design is open loop and fixed point, and shorter still.}
\label{fig:sched}
\end{figure}

Switching the loop back open, so the state is driven from the input sample,
lets the polar conversion start in stage 3 alongside the prediction. The float
design's depth falls from 58 to 32 (Figure~\ref{fig:sched}(b); the
open-loop depth is measured, not drawn). Fixed-point arithmetic then shortens
both chains. The replica's fixed-point \texttt{atan2} and \texttt{sqrt} are each a few cycles rather than 28 and 11, and the closed-loop fixed-point design (step~4) is
\HW{ablfxc}{hDepth} cycles deep, the open-loop replica (step~5)
\HW{ablk}{hDepth}. The replica is shallower than Kiem's reported 23 cycles; his
figure comes from an older tool version and from code that split its
arithmetic into extra stages to meet timing (his Section~4.4.1), so an exact
match is not expected.

\noindent
\textbf{Of DRHE-1's 58 cycles, 26 are the closed loop; the open-loop float
design is still 20 cycles deeper than the replica, and that is the latency of
floating-point operators.} The closed loop is the price of guaranteeing that the encoder
and the decoder can never disagree; at \SI{260}{\nano\second} it is not a price
this application notices (Section~\ref{sec:latency}).

\subsection{Initiation interval: one write too many}
\label{sec:acc_ii}

\paragraph{The difference.} DRHE-1 accepts a new sample every two cycles
($\mathrm{II} = 2$); Kiem's design accepts one every cycle.

\paragraph{The cause, as HLS reports it.} The synthesis log names it:
\begin{quote}\small\ttfamily
Unable to schedule 'load' operation 32 bit ('pp2') on array
\dots s\_prev\_prev\_phase\_3 due to limited memory ports (II = 1).
\end{quote}
Each state array is one block RAM, and a block RAM has two ports. In every cycle
each array must be read once (the old value) and written once (the new one).
DRHE-1 also clears the state at the start of a frame with an \emph{extra} write
on ramp 0 (\texttt{if (r == 0) s\_prev\_prev\_phase[ch][s] = 0;}). That write
only happens once in 192 ramps, but HLS must schedule for the worst case, so
every cycle is scheduled as if three accesses were needed, and three accesses on
two ports take two cycles.

\paragraph{The proof.} Step~3 of the ladder replaces the write with a read that
is ignored on ramp 0 (\texttt{pp2 = first ? 0 : s\_prev\_prev\_phase[ch][s]}),
which clears the state just as well with one read and one write per cycle. The
same float design then schedules at $\mathrm{II} = 1$. Steps 1 and 2, which change only the packer, stay at
$\mathrm{II} = 2$ with the identical message.

\paragraph{What it costs to fix.} $\mathrm{II} = 1$ doubles the theoretical
throughput. In RTL co-simulation, where the step-3 design also reconstructs its
frame losslessly, the measured rate rises from \SI{5.14}{} to
\HW{ablptwor}{thrmeas}\,Gbit/s (\HW{ablptwor}{framecyc} cycles per frame
against \num{61253}); it does not quite double because the 59-cycle pipeline
still drains once per ramp. Step~3 of Table~\ref{tab:abl} also shows the bill: the float arithmetic can no
longer be shared between channels, so DSPs and logic both rise. This is the one step in the ladder that makes the design substantially larger, and it is why DRHE-1's
$\mathrm{II} = 2$ was not a pure loss: with 24 times more throughput than the
sensor needs (Section~\ref{sec:thrinterp}), the halved rate buys a design half
the size in arithmetic.

\subsection{Clock frequency and speed grade}
\label{sec:fmaxinterp}

\paragraph{What it is.} $F_{\max}$ is the fastest clock at which every signal
still settles within one period. Vivado's timing analysis after routing gives
the worst negative slack (WNS): how much time the slowest path has to spare at
the \SI{10}{\nano\second} constraint. The slowest path can then be run at a
period of $10 - \mathrm{WNS}$ nanoseconds.

\paragraph{Why measure it.} Throughput and latency in seconds both depend on
the clock, and the compressor has to share a clock with the FFT and DMA around
it. Kiem ran all his IP on one \SI{100}{\mega\hertz} clock.

\paragraph{The numbers.} Every design meets \SI{100}{\mega\hertz}. The routed
worst paths are:

\paragraph{Against Kiem: now valid, with one correction.} Kiem's design met
\SI{100}{\mega\hertz} on a speed grade $-1$ part, which is slower silicon than
the $-2$ part used here. The replica closes at \HW{ablksgone}{fmax}\,MHz on a
$-1$ part and \HW{ablk}{fmax}\,MHz on a $-2$, so the speed grade is worth about \HW{ablksgone}{sgpct}\,\% of clock for the
same source (HLS also schedules the slower part two stages deeper, so it is not
quite the same netlist). Implemented on a

faster than on the $-2$ part, because HLS scheduled it \HW{ablctrlsgone}{hDepth}
stages deep instead of 58 for the slower silicon --- so it meets Kiem's clock
on Kiem's grade of silicon with room to spare.

\paragraph{One design with little margin.} The fixed-point version of DRHE-1
(Section~\ref{sec:acc_lutff}) closes at only \HW{ablf}{fmax}\,MHz. With the
loop closed and the pipeline only \HW{ablf}{hDepth} stages deep, the path from
the fixed-point arithmetic to the state write has 38 levels of logic, 26 of
them carry chains. That is the cost of a short closed-loop pipeline,
and adopting the fixed-point design would mean either accepting that margin or
asking HLS for one or two more stages, which the replica's schedule shows are
cheap.

\paragraph{The HLS estimate was the wrong way round.} HLS estimated
\SI{81.88}{\mega\hertz} for DRHE and \SI{85.43}{\mega\hertz} for LPC and put
the critical path in a different place from where it really is. Every design
the estimate said would fail passes, with a quarter of the period to spare in
DRHE's case. The advice in earlier drafts to pipeline the float datapath or
replace it with a CORDIC ``to meet timing'' was addressing a problem that does
not exist.

\paragraph{When does it become a problem?} If a design cannot share the system
clock, it needs its own clock and a clock-domain-crossing FIFO on each side,
which is extra design work and extra memory. None of these designs needs one.
Of the four main designs, LPC-$n$ has the least margin, \HW{lpcn}{wns}\,ns; a slower speed grade or a
congested in-system build could consume it.

\section{Latency: three different things with the same name}
\label{sec:latency}

``Latency'' means \emph{how long something waits}. The question is always
\emph{what} waits, and for \emph{what}. Three quantities in this project are
called latency, and they answer different questions. Confusing them is the
easiest way to draw a wrong conclusion, including when comparing with Kiem.
Figure~\ref{fig:latscale} puts all of them on one time axis.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{fig_latency_scale.pdf}
\caption[Latencies on one time axis]{Every latency in this chapter on one
logarithmic time axis. The compressor's own delays (left) are two to five
orders of magnitude shorter than the sensor's time-scales (shaded). The only
compressor delays that approach the sensor's scale are LPC's, because LPC must
see the whole frame before it can emit anything.}
\label{fig:latscale}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Pipeline latency: how long one sample takes to go through}

\paragraph{What it is.} Pipeline latency is the number of clock cycles between
a sample entering the compressor and its compressed bits leaving. For DRHE it
is the pipeline depth, \num{58} cycles, or \SI{580}{\nano\second} at
\SI{100}{\mega\hertz}. Many samples are in flight at once, so this is a delay,
not a rate.

\paragraph{Why measure it.} It fixes two things: how much data is ``inside''
the compressor at any moment, and so how deep any buffering around it must be;
and the smallest possible delay the compressor can add to anything.

\paragraph{Against Kiem: valid.} Kiem reports \SI{230}{\nano\second}, which is
23 cycles. This is his HLS estimate, the same kind of number as ours. He also
measured 31 cycles with an integrated logic analyser, the extra 8 cycles being
the time to fill the first 256-bit output word. Estimate against estimate, this
design is 35 cycles (\SI{350}{\nano\second}) deeper, and
Section~\ref{sec:acc_depth} accounted for them: 26 cycles are the closed loop,
which guarantees the encoder and decoder never disagree, and the rest is the
latency of floating-point operators.

\paragraph{When does it become a problem?} In this application it does not.
\SI{580}{\nano\second} is \SI{0.7}{\percent} of one ramp interval. It would
matter only in a control loop that must react within about a microsecond, and
no radar perception chain works on that time-scale. The difference from Kiem is
real, but it has no practical consequence here.

\subsection{Frame latency: how long a whole frame takes, if it all arrives at once}

\paragraph{What it is.} Frame latency is the co-simulation figure: \num{61253}
cycles for DRHE, \num{325166} for the corrected LPC. As
Section~\ref{sec:numsource} explained, it is measured with the frame fed
back-to-back. It is really a \emph{throughput} measurement expressed in cycles:
the time to process one frame's worth of work at full speed.

\paragraph{What it is not.} It is not a delay the system ever experiences. The
real sensor delivers a frame over \SI{15.07}{\milli\second}. At
\SI{100}{\mega\hertz} DRHE needs \SI{612.5}{\micro\second} of work to process
it, so the compressor is busy \SI{4.1}{\percent} of the time during a frame. The
rest of the time it waits for the next ramp.

\paragraph{Against Kiem: no equivalent number.} Kiem does not report a
per-frame processing time. His \SI{0.08}{\milli\second} is a different
quantity, discussed next.

\subsection{Added latency: how much later the answer arrives because of compression}
\label{sec:addedlat}

This is the latency that matters to the system, and the right answer to the
question ``what does the latency represent?''

\paragraph{What it is.} Without compression, the downstream processor could
start work on a frame the moment its last ramp had been through the range FFT.
With compression, it must wait until the compressor has emitted the last bit of
that frame. The difference is the \emph{added latency}.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{DRHE} is a streaming design. It processes each ramp as that ramp
arrives, so when the last ramp arrives only that one ramp remains:
\num{319} cycles, or \SI{3.2}{\micro\second} at \SI{100}{\mega\hertz}.
\item \textbf{LPC} cannot emit anything until it has seen the whole frame,
because its coefficients are computed from every ramp. When the last ramp
arrives, it still has all of its solve and emit passes to run. That is
\SI{1.24}{\milli\second} for the baseline and \SI{3.01}{\milli\second} for the
corrected design, at \SI{100}{\mega\hertz}.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
This is where the two algorithms differ most. DRHE adds microseconds and LPC
adds milliseconds, a factor of about a thousand.

\paragraph{Kiem's \SI{0.08}{\milli\second}: what it measures.} Kiem's system
figure, \SI{79410}{\nano\second}, is measured from the DMA interrupt that
marks the last raw ramp being \emph{read} from memory to the DMA interrupt that
marks the last compressed word being \emph{written} back. That span includes
the range FFT of the last ramp (\num{1787} cycles, \SI{17.9}{\micro\second}),
DMA transfers and interrupt handling as well as the compressor. It is therefore
an \emph{added-latency} measurement for his whole processing chain, not a
compression time and not a per-frame time. This project has no FFT, DMA or
processor in its test system, so it has no measurement of the same span. The
compressor's share of such a span would be the \SI{3.2}{\micro\second} above.
The comparison with \SI{0.08}{\milli\second} should therefore not be made
directly.

\subsection{When does latency become a problem?}
\label{sec:latproblem}

There are three thresholds, from hardest to softest.

\paragraph{1. The throughput threshold: failure.} If the compressor needs
longer to process one ramp than the sensor takes to produce the next one, a
backlog builds up, and no finite buffer can hold it for ever. Data is lost.
This is the only threshold at which the design stops working, so it is the
first to check. For this sensor the limit is \num{7850} cycles per ramp at
\SI{100}{\mega\hertz}. DRHE uses \num{319}, so it would still keep up at a
clock about 25 times slower. LPC's ingest pass runs at one beat per cycle, so
it also keeps up easily. If frames could be buffered whole, the equivalent
limit would be the frame period, \SI{200}{\milli\second}, which both designs
meet by a very wide margin.

\paragraph{2. The budget threshold: the system's schedule.} The downstream
processor must receive each frame in time to finish its own work before the
next frame arrives. Kiem made this explicit for his sensor. It has a
\SI{50}{\milli\second} cycle and \SI{21}{\milli\second} of acquisition, which
leaves \SI{29}{\milli\second}, and he allocated \SI{15}{\milli\second} of that
to compression. For ColoRadar at \SI{5}{\hertz} the gap after acquisition is
about \SI{185}{\milli\second}, and both designs use a tiny fraction of it.

The budget becomes informative when the designs are \emph{scaled to Kiem's
sensor}, whose frame is 21 times larger (512 bins by 1024 ramps against 128 by
192):

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{DRHE} still processes one ramp at a time. At 512 bins and
$\mathrm{II} = 2$ a ramp takes about \num{1090} cycles, or
\SI{10.9}{\micro\second}, against a ramp interval of about
\SI{20.5}{\micro\second}. It keeps up, and its added latency stays in
microseconds.
\item \textbf{LPC}'s post-ingest passes scale with the frame. Scaling the
measured cycle counts gives roughly \SI{53}{\milli\second} of added latency
at \SI{100}{\mega\hertz}, which is \emph{3.5 times over} Kiem's
\SI{15}{\milli\second} budget. Its frame buffer would need
\SI{67}{\mega bit}. That exceeds the entire block RAM of Kiem's ZU3CG
(\SI{8.0}{\mega bit}) and of the \texttt{xcku5p} (\SI{17.7}{\mega bit},
or \SI{36}{\mega bit} with its UltraRAM), so
it would have to move to external DRAM.
\end{itemize}

\noindent
So on ColoRadar, LPC's latency is acceptable. On a longer-frame sensor like
Kiem's it is the limiting factor, and it is the strongest argument for DRHE in
this thesis. The scaled figures are extrapolations from measured cycle counts,
not measurements. They are accurate enough to show the budget is exceeded,
because the margin is large.

A related, quieter problem is \emph{double buffering}. If one frame follows
another with no idle gap, LPC is still emitting frame $k$ while frame $k+1$ is
arriving. It then needs a second frame buffer, doubling its largest cost.
DRHE has no frame buffer to double.

\paragraph{3. The application threshold: reaction time.} Finally, any added
delay lengthens the time between something happening in front of the vehicle
and the vehicle knowing about it. A physical scale helps. At
\SI{100}{\kilo\metre\per\hour} a car travels \SI{2.78}{\centi\metre} per
millisecond. LPC's \SI{3.0}{\milli\second} is \SI{8.4}{\centi\metre} of
travel. DRHE's \SI{3.2}{\micro\second} is under a tenth of a millimetre. Both
are small next to the frame period itself: a \SI{5}{\hertz} radar is already
up to \SI{200}{\milli\second}, or \SI{5.6}{\metre}, behind reality. Added
latency becomes an application problem when it is a noticeable fraction of the
frame period. LPC on ColoRadar is \SI{1.5}{\percent}, so it is not a problem.
LPC scaled to Kiem's sensor would exceed his entire \SI{50}{\milli\second}
cycle, so it is.


\section{Throughput and initiation interval}
\label{sec:thrinterp}

\paragraph{What they are.} Throughput is how many bits per second the
compressor can accept. Initiation interval (II) is how many cycles it needs
between accepting one input word and the next. With a 128-bit input word at
clock $f$, the \emph{theoretical} throughput is $128 f / \mathrm{II}$. The
\emph{measured} throughput is the bits in a frame divided by the co-simulated
frame time, and is lower because of pipeline fill and drain.

\paragraph{Why measure it.} Throughput tells us whether the compressor keeps
up with the sensor, which is the throughput threshold of
Section~\ref{sec:latproblem}. II is the main thing that sets throughput, and it
is the thing a designer can change.

number with a measured one. Theoretical against theoretical, the gap is exactly
2, which is II~2 against II~1. The remaining \SI{20}{\percent} is per-ramp
pipeline drain. It appears in our measured number, and it would not appear in
Kiem's theoretical one even if his design had it too.

only one set of logic.
\item \textbf{A slower clock.} Dynamic power scales roughly with clock
frequency. A design with 24 times headroom does not need to run at
\SI{100}{\mega\hertz}.
\item \textbf{Larger sensors.} On Kiem's sensor, which needs
\SI{3.2}{Gbit/s}, DRHE's measured \SI{5.1}{Gbit/s} still has 1.6 times
headroom.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Against the architecture's own ideal.} The 128-bit input could in
principle carry 128 bits per cycle. DRHE achieves 51.4, which is
\SI{40}{\percent} of the input width and \SI{80}{\percent} of what II~2 allows.
This is the fairest measure of implementation efficiency, because it needs
nothing external. It also points at the two fixes: the II, which
Section~\ref{sec:acc_ii} traced to one write and removed, and the per-ramp
drain.

\paragraph{When does it become a problem?} When headroom falls below 1. For a
single 4-channel ColoRadar stream that would take a design about 25 times
slower than this one. The practical risk is elsewhere. At II~2, the compressor
accepts data at half the rate the FFT core can deliver it within a ramp, so the
FFT must be allowed to pause (AXI back-pressure), or there must be a small
FIFO between them. One ramp for four channels is \SI{16}{\kilo bit}, about one
BRAM\_18K. At II~1 the question would not arise.

\section{Resources against the device}
\label{sec:resinterp}

\paragraph{Why measure resources at all?} Every resource number answers three
questions. \emph{Does it fit} on the chip that would actually be used, next to
everything else that chip must do? \emph{What does it cost} compared with
another design that does the same job? And \emph{how does it scale} when the
sensor gets bigger? Section~\ref{sec:abl} answered the second. This section
answers the first and third.

\input{tab_device}

\paragraph{It fits Kiem's device.} Every design fits the \texttt{XCZU3CG}, and
DRHE uses fewer of its LUTs than Kiem's own IP does, though more of its
flip-flops, DSPs and block RAM. An earlier draft concluded, from
HLS estimates, that DRHE needed \SI{164}{\percent} of that device's LUTs and
could not be built on it at all; implemented, it needs half. Kiem's complete
system (FFT, DMA, processor interface and compressor) used \num{59353} LUTs, so
everything except his compressor used \num{13461}. DRHE-$n$ in place of his
compressor would bring the system to about \HW{drhen}{sysLUT} LUTs, or
\HW{drhen}{sysLUTpct}\,\% of the device, inside the usual 70--80\,\% guideline
above which placement and routing become congested.

\paragraph{Scaling to the whole sensor.} The designs compress four receive
channels; the ColoRadar cascade has sixteen. Two ways of covering all sixteen
follow from the numbers:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Four DRHE-$n$ instances}, about \HW{drhen}{fourLUT} LUTs and 160
BRAM tiles. That fits the \texttt{xcku5p} (\HW{drhen}{fourLUTpct}\,\% of its
LUTs) but not the \texttt{XCZU3CG}.
\item \textbf{One DRHE-$n$ instance, time-multiplexed.} Its throughput headroom
of 6.2 at \SI{100}{\mega\hertz} over the 16-channel rate makes this possible.
Logic stays at one instance; the state memory grows four-fold to 160 tiles,
which fits the \texttt{xcku5p} (33\,\%) comfortably and the \texttt{XCZU3CG}
only tightly (74\,\% of its block RAM, before the rest of the system is
placed).
\end{itemize}
LPC's memory is its limit: its frame buffer alone is \SI{3.1}{\mebi bit} per
four channels, so all sixteen channels would need about 430 tiles: 90\,\% of
the \texttt{xcku5p} and twice the \texttt{XCZU3CG}. At Kiem's frame size it
could not be held on chip at all
(Section~\ref{sec:latproblem}).

\label{sec:power}

dynamic power is similar to DRHE's but it takes five times as long.

\paragraph{Against the job.} At the sensor's actual rate the compressor is busy
only a few per cent of the time. Energy per frame is therefore the useful
figure: at \SI{5}{frames\per\second}, DRHE-1's \HW{drheone}{Eframe}\,\si{\micro\joule}
per four-channel frame is about \SI{1.7}{\milli\watt} of average switching power (the clock network
keeps toggling between ramps unless it is gated, and is about a ninth of the
dynamic figure),
negligible next to the \SI{0.46}{\watt} the device draws simply by being
switched on. That static power is a property of the development board's large
FPGA, not of the compressor; in a production ASIC or a small device it would be
far lower.

\paragraph{How far to trust it.} These are estimates from routed netlists with
an assumed \SI{12.5}{\percent} toggle rate on every signal, not measurements on
a board and not driven by recorded switching activity. Vivado rates their
confidence as ``medium''. They are good for ranking designs against each other
and for the order of magnitude; a board measurement would be needed for an
absolute figure.

\section{Compression ratio: what counts as good}
\label{sec:crinterp}

\paragraph{What it is.} Compression ratio (CR) is bits in divided by bits out,
measured on real data. It is the reason the compressor exists.

\paragraph{Against the link.} The link sets the requirement. Kiem's sensor
produces \SI{2.4}{Gbit/s} of raw ADC data. To fit a Gigabit Ethernet link
(1000BASE-T1) it needs a CR of 2.4, or 2.7 once protocol headers are included.
The corrected DRHE's 3.75 would clear that. For ColoRadar the picture is
different, and it should be stated plainly: its post-FFT rate for all 16
channels is \SI{0.835}{Gbit/s} while a frame is being acquired. That already
fits under \SI{1}{Gbit/s}, if only just, before compression. After compression
it is \SI{0.223}{Gbit/s}. On this sensor, compression buys \emph{margin} and
room for more channels, not the ability to use Gigabit Ethernet at all.

\paragraph{Against Kiem: not valid.} Kiem's hardware CR was 3.17, on his own
data. The number looks comparable, but it is not. Section~\ref{sec:decomposition}
showed that 1.64 times of this thesis's ratio comes from unused headroom in the
16-bit container. That is a property of how ColoRadar's data is scaled, not of
the algorithm. A CR measured on a different sensor with different scaling
measures a different thing, so a higher or lower number on another dataset says
nothing about which implementation is better. The replication of
Chapter~\ref{ch:replication} makes the point from Kiem's side: on his own
simulated scene his chain reaches his 3.25 only when the residuals are about
the size his dictionary was built for, and much less or much more noise moves
the ratio a long way from it. Chapter~\ref{ch:kiemcompare} goes through each of
his results in turn.

\paragraph{The valid yardsticks.} These are the internal ones:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{No prediction.} The same entropy coder applied to the raw values
gives 3.479 over the same 50 frames (3.489 over the five audit frames). This
is the test of whether prediction helps at all. The original designs failed it;
the corrected designs beat it by \SI{7.8}{\percent} (DRHE) and
\SI{6.3}{\percent} (LPC) (Table~\ref{tab:fifty}).
\item \textbf{The entropy bound.} The order-0 entropy is the least any coder
of this type could spend per value. The fixed Huffman code runs
\SI{12.0}{\percent} above it on the raw codes and \SI{6.2}{\percent} on the
lag-1 DRHE residuals, but \SI{21}{\percent} and more on the corrected
residuals, which are smaller than the dictionary was designed for; a
re-derived dictionary recovers 12 to \SI{18}{\percent} of ratio
(Section~\ref{sec:dictresults}).
\item \textbf{Each other.} DRHE and LPC run on the same data through the same
coder, so their ratios are directly comparable: 3.751 against 3.697.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{When does it become a problem?} When the compressed rate, plus
protocol overhead, no longer fits the link. The caution from
Section~\ref{sec:decomposition} matters here. A sensor whose ADC output filled
the 16-bit container would lose the 1.64 times headroom factor. A rough
estimate, multiplying the sparsity factor (2.13) by the corrected prediction
gain (1.08), puts its ratio near 2.3, which is \emph{below} the 2.4 Kiem's link
requires. That is an estimate from factors measured on ColoRadar, not a
measurement on such a sensor. It shows that a CR of 3.75 on this dataset is not
a guaranteed pass on every sensor.
