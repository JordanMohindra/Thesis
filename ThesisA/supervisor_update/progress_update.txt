THE SHORT VERSION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Since the Thesis A report I have built, verified and characterised complete
hardware implementations of TWO lossless radar compression algorithms - DRHE
and LPC - on the Kintex UltraScale+ FPGA, each with a matching decompressor.
Both reconstruct real radar data perfectly: zero error across 50 frames
(4.9 million complex samples, 157 million bits), confirmed three separate ways.
Both are now also placed and routed in Vivado.

Then I audited the results, and found something I was not expecting.

  *** THE MAIN FINDING ***
  Applied the way the literature describes them, NEITHER PREDICTOR WAS DOING
  ANYTHING USEFUL. Running the same data through the same entropy coder with
  NO PREDICTION AT ALL compressed BETTER than either algorithm - 3.489 against
  3.319 (DRHE) and 3.397 (LPC). Both predictors were making the data
  measurably harder to compress.

  The cause is that our radar is a 12-transmitter TDM-MIMO cascade, and the
  transmitters are interleaved along the axis the algorithms predict along.
  "The previous ramp" is a different physical antenna 11 times out of 12.
  Predicting from the previous ramp of the SAME transmitter fixes it, and
  lifts measured compression from 3.310 to 3.751 for DRHE and 3.385 to 3.697
  for LPC - still perfectly lossless. For DRHE the fix costs 40 extra block
  RAMs (4% of the chip) and NOTHING ELSE: same logic, same clock speed, same
  latency.

I have implemented, verified and synthesised the corrected versions of both
algorithms. I also found and fixed two implementation bugs that would have
shipped, and corrected three factual errors - one in the literature, two in my
own Thesis A report.

1. WHAT I BUILT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE TWO COMPRESSORS, IN PLAIN TERMS

Both algorithms work the same way in outline. They PREDICT what the next radar
sample will be from samples already sent, then transmit only the DIFFERENCE
between the prediction and reality. When the prediction is good the differences
are tiny, and tiny numbers can be written in very few bits. Nothing is thrown
away - the receiver runs the same prediction and adds the difference back - so
the process is exactly lossless.

They differ in how they predict:

  DRHE  converts each sample to magnitude and phase and predicts each with a
        fixed filter. It assumes a target's brightness stays roughly constant
        and its phase advances by a constant step, which is what a target
        moving at constant speed does. It is STREAMING: no buffer needed, and
        it starts producing output immediately.

  LPC   fits a mathematical model to the data instead of assuming one. More
        flexible, but it has to see the whole frame before it can fit the
        model - so it needs a 3.1 Mbit buffer and cannot output anything until
        the frame is complete.

Both then feed their differences into the same entropy coder, which assigns
short bit patterns to common values and long ones to rare values.

DELIVERABLES
  - DRHE compressor and decompressor in Vitis HLS (C++), synthesised, placed and routed for xcku5p
  - LPC compressor and decompressor, likewise
  - TDM-corrected versions of both, kept alongside the originals so every
    comparison is like-for-like
  - A 50-frame testbench driven by real ColoRadar data exported from MATLAB

2. HOW I VERIFIED IT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The single most important property of these compressors is that they are
EXACTLY lossless - a radar that silently loses a weak target to save bandwidth
is not safe. So the verification had to be able to PROVE exactness, not
estimate it. I used a four-stage ladder, each stage checking the one above it
at a lower level:

  1. MATLAB, double precision.  The golden reference. Tells us exactly what
     bits should come out.
  2. HLS C-simulation.  The hardware C++ compiled and run as ordinary
     software. Asks: does it reproduce MATLAB BIT FOR BIT?
  3. RTL co-simulation.  The synthesised hardware, simulated cycle-by-cycle.
     Asks: did synthesis change anything? Also gives exact latency.
  4. On silicon.  Not yet done - needs the board.

Every design passes stages 1-3 with ZERO reconstruction error over all 50
frames.

Agreement with MATLAB is tighter than I set as the pass criterion. For LPC, a
MATLAB model that accounts for AXI packet padding reproduces the hardware bit
count on 50 of 50 frames to within 5e-6 - meaning the underlying bit counts are
identical and the only difference is packet padding.

  FX16          1.00    3.77    1.11   the quantisation step alone

Two points worth making to anyone reading this table:

  * DRHE, LPC and FX16 have identical detection metrics BY CONSTRUCTION, not by

    detector sees identical input. What those rows actually measure is the cost
    of the QUANTISATION step, not of compression.

  * BAQ shows why lossless matters. It reaches a slightly better ratio but

WHAT CHANGED WHEN THE DESIGNS WERE ACTUALLY BUILT. The previous version said
DRHE used 2.5x Kiem's LUTs, would not fit his ZU3EG (actually an XCZU3CG), and
missed timing at 81.9 MHz. All three came from HLS estimates. Implemented,
DRHE-1 uses 0.77x his LUTs, fits his device at half its logic, and closes at
135.5 MHz. LPC is not much cheaper in logic after all: the corrected designs
are within a few per cent of each other.

EVERY DIFFERENCE FROM KIEM, EXPLAINED. I rebuilt his design from his thesis
(his fixed-point types and functions, 58-bit packer, open loop) and went from
our design to it one decision at a time, implementing every step:
  - Block RAM 2x : 32-bit float state words vs his 16-bit (replica = his 10 tiles).
  - DSPs 3x      : every DSP is a float operator; 6x without II=2 channel sharing.
  - II 2 vs 1    : one extra state write per cycle; changed to a read -> II 1.
  - Depth 58/23  : 26 cycles are our closed loop; the rest is float latency.
  - LUT/FF       : mostly the float datapath (69% of LUTs), which buys 0.07% CR;
                   our packer costs ~4,200 LUTs more than his.
One thing does not close: Kiem's own build uses 2.68x the LUTs of the replica.
His speed grade, a literal reading of his packer and his pipeline depth explain
about half of his extra flip-flops and almost none of his extra LUTs. The rest
is most likely the older tool (2022.2) or logic his thesis does not describe;
I report it as unexplained.


4. THE MAIN FINDING: WE WERE PREDICTING ALONG THE WRONG AXIS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
4.1 HOW I FOUND IT

At this point every test passed and the compression ratios matched the
literature, so the obvious conclusion was that the work was done. Before
writing it up I ran one extra check that is not part of any standard
compression evaluation:

    What happens if I remove the predictor entirely and code the raw data
    through the same entropy coder?

Anything that survives is the entropy coder's doing, not the predictor's.

  Configuration              bits/value     CR     vs no prediction
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------
  NO PREDICTION AT ALL          4.586     3.489          -
  DRHE as implemented           4.821     3.319     0.951x  (WORSE)
  LPC as implemented            4.710     3.397     0.974x  (WORSE)

Both predictors were COSTING bits. An independent check confirms it: the
information content (order-0 entropy) of the raw data is 4.094 bits, and of the
DRHE differences 4.538 bits. The predictor was INCREASING the entropy of data
it was supposed to be simplifying.

4.2 WHY

Our radar has 12 transmit antennas that fire one at a time in rotation. When
the data is laid out for a streaming compressor, the "ramp" index is

    m = (chirp loop) x 12 + (transmitter index)

so the transmitter changes EVERY SINGLE RAMP. Ramp m and ramp m-1 were
transmitted by different antennas, in different physical positions. The
difference between them is dominated by WHERE THE TARGET IS IN ANGLE, not by
how fast it is moving - and it jumps around on a period-12 pattern that neither
algorithm can track.

The genuine slow-time neighbour - the one whose difference reflects target
MOTION - is ramp m-12.

  NOTE: this is not a bug in Kiem's work. On a single-transmitter radar, which
  is what his specification addresses, ramp m-1 IS the correct neighbour. The
  error is in applying that specification unmodified to a MIMO sensor of a
  different kind - which is what I did before measuring it.

4.3 WHAT IT LOOKS LIKE ON REAL DATA

Sixteen consecutive ramps of one range bin, run through the same DRHE predictor
twice, changing only the prediction lag:

    predicting from ramp m-1   ->  176 bits  (CR 1.455)
    predicting from ramp m-12  ->   66 bits  (CR 3.879)

for identical information. The underlying reason is in the raw numbers: against
ramp m-1 the target's phase step swings over +/-2.6 radians with no pattern;
against ramp m-12 it is 0.002, -0.040, 0.017, -0.008 - essentially zero,
because it is a stationary target and twelve ramps later the same antenna sees
the same thing.

4.4 THE FIX, AND WHAT IT COSTS

  Design               CR        MaxDiff    LUT      FF     BRAM   Latency
  ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
  DRHE, original    3.30987         0    115,534  57,105     40    61,253
  DRHE, CORRECTED   3.75082         0    115,736  57,263     80    61,253
  LPC, original     3.38497         0     80,528  17,393    256   197,277
  LPC, CORRECTED    3.69681         0     86,298  23,154    192   325,166

  * DRHE: +13.3% compression for 40 block RAMs. Logic, DSPs, clock speed and
    measured frame latency are all unchanged. The extra memory holds one copy
    of the predictor's state per transmitter. About as close to a free
    improvement as hardware gets.

  * LPC: +9.2%, and a genuine trade. Restructuring for the deeper lags happened
    to fix an unrelated bottleneck - block RAM drops 25% and every loop now
    runs at full rate, which the original never achieved. But the frame takes
    1.65x longer. I have identified how to recover most of that.

The improvement is uniform, not an average artefact - the corrected designs
beat their originals on every single one of the 50 frames.


5. SECOND FINDING: WHAT A COMPRESSION RATIO ACTUALLY MEASURES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The same audit answered a question I had not thought to ask: where does the 3.5
come from, if not from prediction?

  * A FACTOR OF 1.63 IS UNUSED CONTAINER HEADROOM. Our quantisation step scales
    the data assuming a full-scale ADC input. The ColoRadar recording sits far
    below that - its peak raw sample is 914 out of a possible 32,767, and after
    processing the peak 16-bit code is 436. ONLY 9.8 OF THE 16 BITS EVER CARRY
    INFORMATION; the other 6.2 are zero in every sample. Any entropy coder
    removes them for free.

  * A FACTOR OF 2.14 IS GENUINE. Radar range-FFT data is sparse - most bins are
    noise near zero - and that is real, transferable compression.

  * THE REST IS PREDICTION, which as implemented was negative and after the
    correction is about 1.08.

  WHY THIS MATTERS BEYOND OUR PROJECT
  It means the compression ratios reported in this field - ours and others' -
  are substantially a property of the SENSOR'S GAIN SETTING rather than of the
  algorithm. A better-scaled recording would compress measurably less on
  identical code. I think reporting a no-prediction baseline alongside any such
  figure should be standard, and as far as I can find, it is not.

  It also points directly at Thesis A Objective 1. We are currently wasting 6.2
  bits of dynamic range at exactly the point where weak targets are being
  crushed into the quantisation noise.


6. TWO BUGS FOUND AND FIXED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Both were invisible on real data and both were caught by the synthetic
edge-case suite. Both would have shipped.

6.1 AN UNREPRESENTABLE VALUE

The entropy coder's largest category holds exactly 2^15 values, which exactly
fills its payload field - leaving no room for the value -32768, which 16-bit
arithmetic can produce. Encoding it produces bit-for-bit the same output as
+32767, and the decoder returns the wrong number. The edge-case test showed a
reconstruction error of 65,535, which is precisely the distance between those
two values.

The fix is to clamp -32768 to -32767 - but clamping alone is not enough. Once
the reconstruction differs from the original by even one bit, the encoder and
decoder start predicting from different histories and DIVERGE, with the error
growing through every remaining ramp. The complete fix is to have the encoder
reconstruct exactly what the decoder will see and predict from THAT.

6.2 A SILENT NUMERICAL FAILURE

LPC's solver needs products of large integers, computed as 128-bit values.
Converting one of those directly to floating point is incorrect above 2^64 in
this toolchain - it returns zero. A determinant of 4.4e20 was converting to
-1.2e18, the wrong sign. Fixed by scaling all the operands down by a common
shift before dividing, which leaves the answer unchanged.

On real data the numbers involved only reach 44 bits, so this was entirely
unreachable with our dataset.

  METHODOLOGICAL POINT WORTH RECORDING
  Fifty frames of real data - 157 million input bits - found neither bug. An
  eight-case synthetic suite found both in minutes. Conversely, the synthetic
  suite would never have found the prediction-axis error, because synthetic
  data has no transmitter structure to get wrong. The two kinds of testing find
  different classes of problem and neither substitutes for the other.


7. CORRECTIONS TO THE RECORD
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Three factual errors surfaced during the audit. I mention them because two are
mine and should be fixed before submission.

  1. A THROUGHPUT FIGURE IN THE LITERATURE IS IN THE WRONG UNITS.
     Kiem reports 11.92 Gbit/s. Read as decimal that is 119.2 bits per clock
     cycle on a 128-bit bus - an odd number. Read as GIBIBITS it is 127.99,
     i.e. exactly 128 - the full bus width at one sample per cycle. The figure
     is binary; its decimal equivalent is 12.80 Gbit/s. Every comparison
     against 11.92 understates the gap by 7.4%. It is also THEORETICAL (bus
     width x clock), not measured - compare it with our theoretical 6.4 Gbit/s
     (a factor of 2, from II), not our measured 5.14. Likewise his 10 BRAM are
     36 Kb tiles = 20 in our units: our baseline uses 2x, not 4x.

  2. OUR "FALSE POSITIVE RATE" IS A FALSE DISCOVERY RATE.
     It divides by the number of REPORTED detections, not by the number of true
     negatives. It is a legitimate metric - arguably the more useful one here -
     but it is not what the name denotes.

  3. TWO CITATIONS IN THE THESIS A EXECUTIVE SUMMARY ARE WRONG.
     The BAQ reference should be Kwok & Johnson, IEEE TGRS vol. 27 no. 4
     pp. 375-383 (1989), on SYNTHETIC aperture radar; the name and page range
     currently cited do not correspond to any such paper. The ColoRadar
     reference should be IJRR vol. 41 no. 4 pp. 351-360 (2022), not vol. 40
     (2021). Also, "simulated aperture radar" should read "synthetic"
     throughout.

Separately: the DRHE description in Thesis A section 2.5.3 does not match what
is actually implemented. It describes differencing RAW ADC samples and coding
with Golomb-Rice. The real algorithm operates on the RANGE-FFT OUTPUT, predicts
in polar coordinates with two IIR filters, and codes with a fixed Huffman
dictionary. Worth reconciling before submission.

9. NEXT STEPS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
In the order I propose tackling them.

HIGH VALUE, LOW EFFORT

  1. ADAPTIVE FX16 SCALE FACTOR. Directly addresses Objective 1, recovers
     roughly 6 bits of dynamic range where weak targets currently die, and
     makes our reported compression ratios reflect the algorithm rather than
     the recording. Probably the single highest-value change remaining.

  2. FIX DRHE'S INITIATION INTERVAL. The design currently accepts one sample
     every TWO cycles instead of every cycle, because of a memory port conflict
     caused by a reset that runs on one ramp in 192. DEMONSTRATED: reading the
     state as zero instead gives II=1 and +71% measured throughput (8.76 Gbit/s),
     but doubles the float arithmetic. Not needed for the sensor (24.6x over).

  3. RECOVER LPC'S LATENCY. The corrected LPC makes two short passes where it
     used to make one long one, and pays a pipeline start-up cost 512 times
     over. Merging the passes should recover most of the 65,000 lost cycles.

MEDIUM EFFORT

  4. MOVE DRHE TO FIXED POINT. Measured: changing only DRHE-1's arithmetic to
     Kiem's fixed point removes 42% of LUTs, 61% of FFs, 67% of DSPs (down to
     exactly Kiem's 44) and half the BRAM, for 0.07% CR. Replaces the earlier
     CORDIC suggestion, which targeted a timing problem that does not exist.

  5. RE-DERIVE THE HUFFMAN DICTIONARY. After the correction, 86% of differences
     fall into the three cheapest categories, which is a more concentrated
     distribution than the current fixed dictionary assumes.

  6. PHASE 4 ON THE BOARD. Place-and-route is done; measured power, in-system
     timing and end-to-end latency remain.

IF TIME ALLOWS

  7. Benchmark against NXP CP4/CP8 and AdaRadar, completing Objective 2.
  8. Check whether the prediction-axis issue has an analogue in
     Doppler-division or code-division MIMO.


10. RISKS AND OPEN QUESTIONS

  * BOARD ACCESS IS THE CRITICAL PATH FOR PHASE 4. Everything else can proceed
    in simulation, but power and real timing cannot.

  * TIMING CLOSURE IS NO LONGER A RISK. Every design meets 100 MHz after

  * HARDWARE NUMBERS ARE POST-ROUTE, NOT SILICON. Power is a vectorless
    estimate; in-system routing could be slightly worse than out of context.

  * SCOPE. Objective 2 is only partly done. I would rather finish the
    quantisation work (Objective 1) properly than add two more algorithms to
    the comparison - but that is a judgement call worth discussing.
